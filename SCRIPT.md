**Slide 4 — A method for finding patterns of meaning** *(pages 4–7, 4 clicks)* — ~40 s

> **[on 1]** Thematic analysis does one thing: it finds themes — patterns of
> meaning — across a whole dataset.
>
> **[click → 2]** Braun and Clarke described it in 2006 as poorly demarcated
> and rarely acknowledged, yet used absolutely everywhere. Their paper set out
> to fix that.
>
> **[click → 3]** It worked — a bit too well. That paper became one of the most
> cited methods papers of the last twenty years.
>
> **[click → 4]** And that's where the trouble started. Thousands of studies
> *cite* it while *doing* something completely different. The diagram on the
> right is the easy part. How you get from the top to the bottom is the whole
> argument.

*→ Click to slide 5.*

---

**Slide 5 — Three families of thematic analysis** *(pages 8–12, 5 clicks)* — ~50 s

> **[on 1]** Because "thematic analysis" isn't one method. It's a family, laid
> out on a spectrum from positivist on the left to fully qualitative on the
> right.
>
> **[click → 2]** **Coding reliability TA.** A fixed codebook, several coders
> working independently, and an inter-rater reliability score. Themes are
> decided before coding even starts.
>
> **[click → 3]** **Codebook TA** — framework analysis, template analysis. Still
> a structured template, but qualitative values underneath. Built for teams and
> deadlines.
>
> **[click → 4]** **Reflexive TA.** No codebook at all. Coding is organic. And
> the researcher's subjectivity is treated as a resource — not a threat to be
> controlled.
>
> **[click → 5]** The mistake is not picking the wrong one. The mistake is
> blending them without noticing — citing reflexive TA, then reporting a
> Cohen's Kappa.

*→ Click to slide 6.*

---

**Slide 6 — Why "reflexive"?** *(pages 13–17, 5 clicks)* — ~35 s

> **[on 1]** So why *reflexive*? Because the analysis sits at an intersection.
> The dataset — **[click → 2]** the theory you bring to it — **[click → 3]**
> and your own analytic skill as a researcher.
>
> **[click → 4]** The analysis lives in the middle. And no two researchers
> arrive at that middle the same way, so reproducibility is not the goal.
>
> **[click → 5]** Change the researcher and the analysis legitimately changes.
> That isn't a flaw in the method. That *is* the method.

*→ Click to slide 7.*

---

**Slide 7 — Themes are an output, never an input** *(pages 18–20, 3 clicks)* — ~25 s

> **[on 1]** Which leads to the line they repeat most often: themes do not
> passively emerge. The passive story says themes lie in the data and you go
> and fetch them.
>
> **[click → 2]** The reflexive story says you *generate* them — you build them
> out of codes.
>
> **[click → 3]** They took this seriously enough to rename phase three of their
> own method, just to kill the metaphor. Which brings us to the phases
> themselves — Meherab.

*→ Click to the Part II transition card and step aside.*

## PART II — Meherab Hossain (2305108)
### The six-phase process · 3:00 · PDF pages 21–38

---

**Slide 8 — Part II transition** *(page 21)* — ~10 s

> Thanks, Arafat. If themes are generated rather than found, then the obvious
> question is: generated *how*? Six phases — and they are a loop, not a
> pipeline.

*→ Click to slide 9.*

---

**Slide 9 — Six phases** *(pages 22–29, 8 clicks)* — ~80 s

> **[on 1]** Braun and Clarke propose six phases — both as a way of *doing*
> analysis and a way of *learning* to do it.
>
> **[click → 2]** **Phase one, familiarisation.** Read and re-read. Transcribe
> it yourself if you can. Write notes as you go. The temptation is to skim this
> — don't.
>
> **[click → 3]** **Phase two, systematic coding.** Work through the entire
> dataset, tagging anything relevant with a short label that makes sense on its
> own.
>
> **[click → 4]** **Phase three, generating themes.** Cluster codes that share
> a meaning. Each cluster needs a central organising concept.
>
> **[click → 5]** **Phase four, reviewing.** Test each candidate twice — against
> its own extracts, then against the whole dataset. Split it, merge it, or drop
> it.
>
> **[click → 6]** **Phase five, defining and naming.** If you can't describe a
> theme's scope in two sentences, it isn't one theme yet.
>
> **[click → 7]** **Phase six, writing.** Writing *is* analysis. It starts at
> phase one and never really stops.
>
> **[click → 8]** And now the important part — the arrows go both ways. Phase
> four regularly throws you back to re-code. Byrne calls the six phases
> guidelines, rather than rules.

*→ Click to slide 10.*

---

**Slide 10 — From one sentence of transcript to one theme** *(pages 30–34, 5 clicks)* — ~65 s

> **[on 1]** Let me make that concrete, using Byrne's worked example —
> interviews with school teachers about student wellbeing. Here's a single data
> item. One teacher, one sentence.
>
> **[click → 2]** From that one sentence you might interpret three codes. The
> guidelines lack clarity. They're not relatable for students. The indicators
> are never actually used.
>
> **[click → 3]** Across eleven interviews, codes like these start clustering —
> not by topic, but by shared meaning. This one joins them: teachers falling
> back on common sense.
>
> **[click → 4]** And the cluster becomes a theme: *incompletely theorised
> agreements*. The central organising concept is that teachers act on student
> wellbeing without any shared theory of what wellbeing actually is.
>
> **[click → 5]** Notice what happened. The theme name is not a summary of the
> topic. Nobody in the interviews said that phrase. It's an interpretation the
> researcher had to build.

*→ Click to slide 11.*

---

**Slide 11 — A code is one facet** *(pages 35–38, 4 clicks)* — ~25 s

> **[on 1]** That's the difference between a code and a theme. A code captures
> one facet.
>
> **[click → 2]** A theme has a central concept with several facets hanging off
> it. A "theme" built from a single code is usually still just a code.
>
> **[click → 3]** And phase four is where this shows. Byrne's first map had four
> candidate themes. One was too dense to defend, so he broke it apart and
> reinterpreted it. The final map had five — three of which didn't exist before
> the review.
>
> **[click → 4]** Revision isn't a sign of failure. It's the method working.
> Dipto will take it from here.

*→ Click to the Part III transition card and step aside.*

## PART III — Dipto Debnath (2305111)
### Decisions, pitfalls, quality · 3:00 · PDF pages 39–57

---

**Slide 12 — Part III transition** *(page 39)* — ~10 s

> Thanks, Meherab. The method is flexible — and that flexibility has a price.
> Every choice has to be declared, and there are ten well-documented ways to get
> this wrong.

*→ Click to slide 13.*

---

**Slide 13 — Four decisions you have to declare** *(pages 40–45, 6 clicks)* — ~45 s

> **[on 1]** Reflexive TA is theoretically flexible — but it is never
> atheoretical. There are four positions you have to state.
>
> **[click → 2]** **Epistemology** — essentialist, where language reflects
> reality, or constructionist, where language helps build it.
>
> **[click → 3]** **Orientation** — experiential, staying with what participants
> mean, or critical, asking what produced those meanings.
>
> **[click → 4]** **Direction** — inductive, driven by the data, or deductive,
> driven by existing theory.
>
> **[click → 5]** **Level of meaning** — semantic, on the surface, or latent,
> underneath it.
>
> **[click → 6]** And notice the knobs almost never sit at an end. Byrne's study
> was mostly inductive with a deductive check, coded semantically *and*
> latently — and crucially, he said so in writing.

*→ Click to slide 14.*

---

**Slide 14 — Ten documented problems** *(pages 46–51, 6 clicks)* — ~60 s

> **[on 1]** In 2020 Braun and Clarke catalogued ten problems they kept finding
> in papers that cited them. Three are worth your time.
>
> **[click → 2]** **Assuming TA is one thing** — a paper citing four
> incompatible TA authors in one sentence. Fix: name your variant and stay
> inside it.
>
> **[click → 3]** **Citing without reading.** "We followed Braun and Clarke" —
> followed immediately by a codebook and a Kappa. They have never recommended
> either.
>
> **[click → 4]** **Topics dressed up as themes** — themes named *Benefits
> of*, *Barriers to*. That's the interview guide, not an analysis.
>
> **[click → 5]** Here's the difference. "Barriers to talking about stress" is a
> bucket for everything said on a topic.
>
> **[click → 6]** Whereas this is a shared-meaning theme. It makes a claim. It's
> something you could actually disagree with.

*→ Click to slide 15.*

---

**Slide 15 — So what counts as quality?** *(pages 52–54, 3 clicks)* — ~45 s

> **[on 1]** So what does good practice look like? A named and justified variant.
> Stated theoretical assumptions — even for inductive work. Themes with a
> central organising concept. And extracts that are analysed, not just
> displayed.
>
> **[click → 2]** And here's the part people find hardest. Inter-rater
> reliability, consensus between coders, saturation borrowed from grounded
> theory, the absence of researcher influence — none of these count.
>
> **[click → 3]** In reflexive TA, chasing unbiased coding isn't just
> unnecessary, it's incoherent, because subjectivity is the instrument. The
> published toolkit is a fifteen-point checklist from 2006 and twenty critical
> questions for reviewers from 2020.

*→ Click to slide 16.*

---

**Slide 16 — The takeaway** *(page 55)* — ~25 s

> **[on 1]** Braun and Clarke describe what they've given researchers as a
> compass and a map — a direction of travel, not a destination. The six phases
> are a loop you walk more than once. The themes are things you build rather
> than find. And the researcher — visibly, deliberately, on the record — is
> part of the result.
>
> Thank you.

*→ Click twice to the references, then the closing slide, and stop.*

## Rehearsal notes

- **The clicker is part of the performance.** On the six-phase loop and the
  transcript-to-theme slide, land each click on the sentence it illustrates.
  Clicking ahead of your words is the single easiest way to lose the effect.
- **Count your clicks before you go on.** Part I: 18 clicks. Part II: 17.
  Part III: 18.
- **The transition cards give you a free beat.** Click onto the card, say your
  one-line hand-off, walk to your position, then click on.
- **If you are running long**, the compressible bits are slide 2 (the roadmap)
  and the last overlay of slide 15. Never compress the animated builds.
- **There is no Q&A**, so end cleanly on "Thank you" and stop — don't trail off
  into an invitation for questions.
